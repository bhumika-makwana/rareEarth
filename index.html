Essay 01 · Scales

Bits to atoms, one power of ten at a time

A bit is the same size everywhere. Matter is not. Why the move from software into the physical world is not one leap but a ladder of them.

In 1977 Charles and Ray Eames made a nine-minute film that begins with a couple on a picnic blanket by the lake in Chicago. Every ten seconds the camera pulls back by a factor of ten, out past the city, the planet and the galaxy, and then dives back in through the man's hand, past skin and cells, into the nucleus of a single carbon atom.

The film is usually remembered for its sense of size. Its deeper lesson is that every jump changes the rules. Gravity holds galaxies together and does almost nothing to a protein. Electromagnetism binds molecules and is irrelevant to the orbit of the Moon. Inside a cell, things move by crowding, diffusion and noise; at the size of a person, by friction and inertia. Each power of ten has its own physics, and with it its own instruments, its own clock and its own idea of what a measurement is.

Thesis

The bottleneck moves as you zoom.

At the smallest scales, we can imagine more than we can make. In the middle, we have the machines but not the experience. At the largest scales, we can see more than we can act on. Wherever the bottleneck sits, the value sits with it.

Software ate the world by ignoring scale. The next great companies will be built by those who understand it. So when we look at any technology, don't just ask whether it is bits or atoms. Ask which power of ten it lives at, what is hard there, and who is solving it.
